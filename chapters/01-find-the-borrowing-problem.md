# Find the borrowing problem

Suppose a neighborhood tool library wants more people to borrow its portable bicycle repair kit. At a weekend table, eight apartment residents stop to talk. Six say lugging tools home is hard. Two say they have borrowed tools before. Somebody writes “75% of residents want our kit” on the planning board. The arithmetic describes something. The sentence describes something else.

This chapter teaches you to turn a loose customer complaint into a research question, propose segments you can distinguish, and say what a small convenience sample cannot tell you. All interviews and quantities in this book are fictional.

## Ask a question that could change the offer

“Do you like this idea?” makes saying yes easy and teaches little about behavior. Ask about a recent specific repair: what did you need, what did you use instead, what part was hard, and when did you abandon the plan? Then ask about practical constraints. A borrower walking home with a repair kit has a different problem from someone who cannot get to the pickup counter at all.

Interview records can suggest a reason to investigate. They cannot quietly become evidence about people who were never approached. This table attracted visitors near one branch in one time window. Even if everyone answered honestly, their visits were not a random draw from neighborhood residents.

| Question the toy notes actually answer | Result | What remains unknown |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Did these respondents say carrying tools was difficult? | 6 of 8 | How common that problem is outside this table. |
| Had these respondents previously borrowed tools? | 2 of 8 | Whether they would borrow under the new offer. |
| Which respondents gave both answers? | Not recorded | The overlap could be zero, one or two. |

If six people are in the first set and two in the second, the largest overlap is two. The smallest is zero because the two groups can occupy all eight without sharing a person. Do not create a cross-tab that the notes did not preserve.

## Segment by a difference in the job

Segmentation organizes potential customers around differences that matter for a workable offer. Do not label a population with an adjective and call that design.

| Candidate segment | Need to investigate | Substitute worth asking about |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nearby repair-ready renters without storage | Access to tools for a specific small job | Borrow from a neighbor |
| People with tools who need help diagnosing a problem | Expertise rather than kit access | Pay a shop or attend a repair session |
| People who cannot reach the staffed window | Access at a usable time and place | Postpone the repair or shop elsewhere |

These are hypotheses, not permanent identities or disjoint categories. A person can be in different groups for different repairs. Choose the next evidence you can gather responsibly: record whether an attendee already has a specific job, which pickup times are workable, and the substitute they used last time. Do not collect details you do not need.

## Try it

Write one question to distinguish “no tools” from “no time.” Explain how the answer changes the proposed service. Then list two groups the table may miss.

**Check.** “When was the last time you could have picked up a repair tool, and what did you do instead?” invites a specific constraint. A daytime table can miss people working that shift or unable to travel to the branch. An evening kit pickup and a second staffed location solve different problems. The original 6/8 figure does not select either.
